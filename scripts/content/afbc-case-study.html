High Bed Temperature in an AFBC Boiler: Understanding Bed-Coil Heat Transfer Limitations

How can an AFBC boiler experience high bed temperature even when the combustion-air system is operating?

Bed temperature is a critical operating parameter in an Atmospheric Fluidized Bed Combustion (AFBC) boiler. Maintaining the bed within its recommended operating range is essential for stable combustion, effective heat transfer, and reliable boiler performance.

In this case study, Boilertech Energy Services shares findings from a field investigation into an AFBC boiler experiencing elevated bed temperature during rated-load operation.

1. The Operating Challenge

The boiler was designed to operate at a bed temperature of approximately 850–900°C. During field observations, the bed temperature reached around 950°C at rated load.

An important operating trend was observed: bed temperature reduced significantly when the boiler load was reduced but remained high at rated load.

This behaviour indicated that the heat generated within the combustion bed could be exceeding the effective heat-removal capacity of the immersed bed evaporator coils at full load.

The investigation therefore focused on the relationship between bed-coil immersion, effective heat-transfer surface, refractory coverage, stud configuration, fuel characteristics, and combustion-air conditions.

2. Why Is Bed-Coil Heat Transfer Important?

In an AFBC boiler, the immersed evaporator coils absorb heat from the fluidized bed and transfer it to the boiler water/steam circuit.

The effective heat pickup depends on several factors:

  • Effective coil surface exposed to the fluidized bed.
  • Expanded bed height and coil immersion.
  • Bed fluidization and solids circulation.
  • Tube surface condition and stud configuration.
  • Refractory coverage over the coils.
  • Fuel properties and heat release within the bed.

If the effective heat absorbed by the bed evaporator is insufficient for the heat released during combustion, the bed temperature can rise beyond its intended operating range.

Importantly, increasing combustion airflow alone may not resolve the underlying problem. The heat-transfer capacity, air distribution, fuel feed, and fluidization conditions must be assessed together.

3. Field Findings: Bed-Coil Immersion Assessment

The investigation considered the reported operating fuel mixture of 80% coal and 20% char.

The bed-coil immersion assessment indicated the following:

At the existing operating condition, the effective immersion length was estimated to be 792 m against a requirement of 946 m.

The calculated shortfall was:

946 − 792 = 154 m

This represents an approximate 16.3% shortfall relative to the stated requirement.

The assessment highlighted how operating bed height and refractory coverage can influence the effective heat-transfer surface available under rated-load conditions.

4. What Factors Can Reduce Bed-Coil Heat Pickup?

A. Reduced bed-coil immersion

When the expanded bed height is lower than required, a smaller portion of the coil remains in effective contact with the fluidized solids. This can reduce heat absorption from the bed.

B. Excessive refractory coverage

Refractory applied beyond the intended design area can shield the coil surface from the fluidized bed. The resulting reduction in effective heat transfer may affect temperature control at higher loads.

C. Stud erosion or incorrect stud configuration

Studs influence the effective heat-transfer surface and interaction between the coil and the fluidized bed. Erosion, missing studs, or deviations from the specified pattern may reduce thermal performance.

D. Fuel characteristics

Changes in calorific value, moisture, ash content, particle size, and coal-to-char ratio can influence heat release and combustion behaviour. Actual fuel properties should be compared with the original boiler design assumptions.

E. Combustion-air distribution

Uneven primary-air distribution, blocked air nozzles, or local variations in fuel feed can contribute to uneven bed temperatures. These conditions should be investigated alongside bed-coil performance.

5. Evaluating the Improvement Options

The field assessment identified several potential measures for improving bed-coil heat pickup.

  • Refractory optimization: Retain refractory protection where required by design, particularly around fuel nozzles, while avoiding unnecessary coverage of heat-transfer surfaces.
  • Stud-pattern review: Assess the condition of existing studs and evaluate the proposed 5 × 6 configuration against the original design and verified thermal calculations.
  • Bed-height assessment: Evaluate whether the required expanded bed height can be maintained safely at rated load.
  • Combustion-air verification: Confirm FD fan performance, wind-box pressure, air distribution, and fluidization conditions.
  • Fuel assessment: Verify the current fuel mixture and properties against the boiler's design basis.

The reported Performance Guaranteed Test indicated that a 1,000 mm expanded bed height with minimum refractory application could provide an effective immersion length of approximately 948 m. However, the estimated 887 m associated with the proposed 5 × 6 stud pattern remained below the stated 946 m requirement when compared on the supplied figures.

Therefore, the final modification must be based on a verified heat-transfer calculation, rather than stud pattern or immersion length alone.

6. What Should Engineers Monitor During Troubleshooting?

For a meaningful assessment, the following operating parameters should be recorded at different boiler loads:

  • Bed temperature at representative locations.
  • Boiler load and steam generation.
  • Expanded bed height and bed pressure drop.
  • Wind-box pressure and actual combustion-air flow.
  • Fuel feed rate and fuel characteristics.
  • FD fan operating point and motor load.
  • Flue-gas oxygen and carbon monoxide.
  • Unburnt carbon in ash, where available.
  • Bed-coil condition and refractory coverage during shutdown inspection.

Comparing these parameters at part load and rated load helps engineers distinguish between inadequate heat-transfer capacity, combustion maldistribution, and other operating constraints.

7. Key Technical Takeaway

High bed temperature is not always an airflow problem. It can also indicate a mismatch between heat released in the combustion bed and the effective heat-removal capacity of the bed evaporator.

In this case, the relationship between boiler load, bed temperature, expanded bed height, and calculated coil immersion provided an important direction for the investigation.

A systematic assessment of heat-transfer surfaces, refractory coverage, stud condition, fuel properties, and combustion-air distribution is essential before finalizing corrective measures.

The effectiveness of any modification should subsequently be confirmed through controlled commissioning and performance measurements at rated load.
